# If you are working in Bura as an ER trauma surgeon, what kind of patients arrive to you?

Working in **“Bara”**—Chris Hani Baragwanath Academic Hospital in Soweto, Johannesburg—as an ER/trauma surgeon, you would see an extremely high volume of severe, often life-threatening injuries. The trauma service treats thousands of priority-one (critically injured) patients each year, with penetrating violence a major part of the workload.[^1_1]

## Typical patients

- **Gunshot wounds**: A large share of penetrating trauma; injuries may involve the chest, abdomen, limbs, head/neck, or multiple body regions. Gunshot wounds account for more than a third of penetrating injuries treated at Bara’s trauma centre.[^1_1]
- **Stab wounds**: Commonly from assaults, including chest and abdominal injuries that can cause lung, heart, liver, bowel, or major-vessel damage.[^1_1]
- **Assault injuries**: Blunt trauma from beatings, as well as penetrating injuries.[^1_1]
- **Road-traffic crashes**: Motor-vehicle, pedestrian, and motorcycle collisions causing blunt polytrauma—head, chest, abdominal, pelvic, spine, and limb injuries.[^1_2][^1_1]
- **Falls and blunt trauma**: High-impact falls and workplace or other blunt-force injuries, with concern for internal bleeding and organ injury even when external wounds are limited.[^1_3][^1_2]
- **Complex fractures and polytrauma**: Patients with several severe injuries at once, often needing coordinated surgery, critical care, and rehabilitation.[^1_4][^1_2]


## What you would do

Your work would center on rapid triage, resuscitation, hemorrhage control, and emergency operations. Examples include chest tubes for collapsed lungs, laparotomy for abdominal gunshot or stab injuries, thoracotomy for major chest/lung or cardiac injury, and damage-control surgery for unstable, massively bleeding patients.[^1_3][^1_1]

In practice, a shift could move from an unstable patient with thoracic stab wounds needing immediate thoracotomy, to laparotomies for abdominal gunshot or stab wounds, to victims of crashes and assaults—often with limited time and high acuity.[^1_1]

## Patient flow

Patients generally arrive by ambulance after prehospital triage or are brought directly by family or bystanders. The most severely injured are prioritized immediately; trauma activations are triggered by signs such as severe bleeding, penetrating torso injuries, major crashes, unconsciousness, or unstable vital signs.[^1_5][^1_6]

So, compared with a typical ER, Bara’s trauma service is especially defined by **high-volume penetrating trauma from interpersonal violence**, alongside major blunt trauma from road crashes and falls.[^1_1]

# Can you give percentages to different injury causes and then resulting injury types for each cause?

Yes—but the percentages depend on which Bara (Chris Hani Baragwanath Academic Hospital, CHBAH) trauma population you mean. The best available figures separate **all priority-one trauma patients** from **penetrating-trauma patients**, so the mix looks very different.

## All priority-one trauma patients

A CHBAH trauma-registry study of 377 priority-one patients from January–June 2022 found interpersonal violence dominated the adult caseload, while burns and falls dominated among children.[^2_1]


| Cause | Share of all priority-one patients | Typical resulting injuries |
| :-- | --: | :-- |
| **Stabbing** | **32.63%** (123/377) | Chest wounds with haemothorax/pneumothorax; abdominal wounds injuring liver, spleen, bowel, or major vessels; limb vascular or tendon injuries. [^2_1][^2_2] |
| **Gunshot wounds** | **14.59%** (55/377) | Penetrating chest, abdominal, head, limb, and vascular injuries; higher-energy tissue destruction and multi-cavity injuries than stab wounds. [^2_1][^2_3] |
| **Assault / blunt interpersonal violence** | **13.53%** (51/377) | Head and facial injuries, soft-tissue wounds, fractures, and occasionally blunt abdominal or chest trauma. [^2_1][^2_2] |
| **Burns** | Especially important in children: **28.07%** of paediatric priority-one cases (16/57) | Thermal burns, inhalation injury, fluid loss, and infection risk. [^2_1] |
| **Falls from height** | **22.81% of paediatric priority-one cases** (13/57); also a major adult blunt-trauma mechanism | Head injury, spinal injuries, pelvic and long-bone fractures, solid-organ injury. [^2_1][^2_4] |
| **Pedestrian–vehicle collisions** | **17.84% of paediatric priority-one cases** (10/57) | Polytrauma: traumatic brain injury, chest/abdominal injuries, pelvic fractures, femur and other long-bone fractures. [^2_1] |

For the full cohort, the study reported that only **5.57%** required emergency surgery, while **33.42%** were admitted to wards—meaning most priority-one arrivals need urgent assessment and stabilization rather than immediate theatre.[^2_1]

## Penetrating trauma specifically

Among **4,697 penetrating-trauma patients triaged priority one** at CHBAH from 2011–2016, stabbings overwhelmingly predominated.[^2_2][^2_5]


| Cause | Share of penetrating priority-one patients | Typical resulting injuries |
| :-- | --: | :-- |
| **Stab wounds** | **71.8%** overall; **73.1%** in the reported injury-mechanism breakdown (3,437/4,697) | Thoracic injury is the commonest body region; haemothorax is the most likely consequence. Other common injuries include pneumothorax, cardiac wounds, diaphragmatic injury, abdominal visceral injury, and limb vascular injury. [^2_5][^2_2] |
| **Gunshot wounds** | The next largest penetrating mechanism, comprising most of the remaining cases; the study reports stabbings followed by gunshots but does not give a single clean percentage in the available excerpt | More severe tissue cavitation, vascular disruption, bowel injury, haemothorax/haemoperitoneum, head and spinal injury, and a greater need for damage-control surgery. [^2_5][^2_3] |

The same penetrating-trauma study found **92.1%** of victims were Black African males, mostly aged 29–40, and that the **thorax** was the most commonly affected body area.[^2_5][^2_2]

## Important caveat

These are **not universal, fixed percentages**. They vary by year, triage category, whether you count all ER trauma or only admissions, and whether children are included. For example, an older Johannesburg surveillance dataset found assault at **38.2%** and road-traffic injuries at **31.6%**, while a mixed South African trauma dataset reported falls at **32.37%**, assault at **27.44%**, transport-related injury at **22.52%**, and burns at **8.01%**.[^2_4][^2_2]

For a practical “Bara ER trauma surgeon” picture, the most defensible summary is: **roughly one-third stabbings, one-seventh gunshot wounds, and one-seventh blunt assaults among recent priority-one patients**, with burns, falls, and pedestrian collisions especially prominent in children.[^2_1]

# What are the typical metrics surgeons follow and use for prioritization in these kind of injuries?

In a high-volume South African trauma unit, surgeons prioritize by **physiologic instability first, anatomy and mechanism second, and resource availability third**. The immediate question is not “which injury looks worst?” but “which patient will die or lose a limb in the next minutes to hours without intervention?”

## First-pass prioritization: ABCDE

The initial assessment follows **ABCDE**: Airway with cervical-spine protection, Breathing, Circulation with hemorrhage control, Disability/neurologic status, and Exposure/environmental control.[^3_1]


| Priority signal | Why it matters |
| :-- | :-- |
| Airway obstruction, stridor, declining consciousness | Requires immediate airway management; precedes detailed imaging. [^3_2][^3_1] |
| Respiratory distress, absent breath sounds, tracheal deviation, SpO2 below 90% | Suggests tension pneumothorax, massive haemothorax, or failed ventilation; needs immediate decompression. [^3_2][^3_1] |
| Active external hemorrhage, weak pulses, delayed capillary refill, SBP below 90 mmHg | Indicates shock; direct pressure, tourniquet where appropriate, and hemorrhage control take precedence. [^3_2][^3_1] |
| GCS below 13, unequal pupils, focal deficit | Signals traumatic brain injury or neurologic deterioration; affects airway, CT, and operative decisions. [^3_2][^3_3] |
| Exposed organs, major burns, hypothermia | Requires exposure assessment and prevention of heat loss while controlling the primary threat. [^3_1] |

South Africa’s Cape Triage Score uses respiratory rate, heart rate, systolic blood pressure, temperature, AVPU consciousness, mobility, and trauma status to assign **red (immediate), orange (very urgent), yellow, green, or blue/dead** categories.[^3_2][^3_4]

## Hemodynamic and shock metrics

For a penetrating chest or abdominal injury, the surgeon constantly asks whether the patient is bleeding internally and whether the bleeding can wait for CT or needs theatre now.


| Metric | Typical concern threshold | Use |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Systolic blood pressure** | Below 90 mmHg in adults | Marker of overt shock; supports immediate resuscitation and operative planning. [^3_5][^3_2] |
| **Heart rate** | Above 120 bpm | Early compensatory sign, especially with penetrating torso trauma. [^3_5][^3_6] |
| **Shock index** | HR divided by SBP; rising values suggest occult shock | A rapid bedside indicator of hemodynamic instability. [^3_3] |
| **Glasgow Coma Scale** | Lower score, especially GCS below 13 | Guides airway protection and neurosurgical urgency. [^3_3][^3_2] |
| **Respiratory rate** | Abnormally high or low | Part of physiologic triage and a component of trauma-severity scoring. [^3_3][^3_2] |
| **FAST ultrasound** | Positive fluid, particularly in multiple regions | Supports intraperitoneal bleeding and can trigger theatre or massive transfusion. [^3_5][^3_6] |
| **Response to fluids/blood** | Non-responder or transient responder | Suggests ongoing hemorrhage; pushes toward operative control rather than continued resuscitation. [^3_7] |

## Laboratory and perfusion metrics

Blood pressure and pulse can look deceptively normal in young patients, so surgeons use tissue-perfusion markers to detect hidden shock. Base deficit and lactate are considered more reliable indicators of resuscitation adequacy and end-organ perfusion than vital signs alone.[^3_8]


| Metric | High-risk finding | Interpretation |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Lactate** | Above 2.5–4 mmol/L | Tissue hypoperfusion and oxygen debt; higher values and poor clearance correlate with mortality. [^3_9][^3_8][^3_10] |
| **Base deficit** | Worse than -6 mEq/L | Metabolic acidosis from shock; strongly associated with hemorrhage severity and mortality. [^3_9][^3_3] |
| **pH** | Below 7.25 | Severe acidemia; supports urgent hemorrhage control and damage-control resuscitation. [^3_9] |
| **INR / coagulation profile** | INR at or above 1.5 | Trauma-induced coagulopathy; increases bleeding risk and transfusion needs. [^3_9][^3_10] |
| **Hemoglobin** | Falling or low relative to clinical status | Helps assess blood loss, but a normal early value does not exclude major hemorrhage. [^3_7] |
| **Temperature** | Hypothermia | Part of the lethal triad with acidosis and coagulopathy; worsens clotting and must be corrected. [^3_11][^3_6] |
| **Calcium** | Low during transfusion | Important in massive transfusion and damage-control resuscitation. [^3_8] |

## Massive-transfusion triggers

A commonly used **ABC score** assigns one point each for penetrating torso injury, SBP below 90 mmHg, pulse above 120 bpm, and positive FAST; a score of **2 or more** supports activation of a massive transfusion protocol.[^3_5][^3_6]

Other red flags include lactate above 4 mmol/L, base deficit worse than -6 mEq/L, pH below 7.25, INR at or above 1.5, or FAST positivity in two or more regions. Once activated, care focuses on balanced blood-product resuscitation, minimizing excessive crystalloid, preventing hypothermia/acidosis/coagulopathy, and rapid surgical hemorrhage control.[^3_7][^3_9][^3_10][^3_8]

## Injury-pattern metrics

Surgeons also classify patients by **anatomical severity** and **physiologic severity**, because a stable patient with a dangerous injury pattern may deteriorate rapidly.

- **Injury Severity Score (ISS):** An anatomical score based on the location and severity of injuries; higher scores indicate more extensive trauma.[^3_3]
- **Revised Trauma Score (RTS):** A physiologic score using systolic blood pressure, respiratory rate, and GCS.[^3_3]
- **TRISS:** Combines ISS, RTS, age, and injury mechanism to estimate probability of survival; it is more useful for benchmarking and audit than for minute-to-minute ER triage.[^3_1]
- **Penetrating torso injury:** Treated as a high-risk mechanism because it can cause non-compressible hemorrhage in the chest, abdomen, or pelvis.[^3_12][^3_5]
- **Positive FAST plus instability:** A particularly urgent combination, often leading directly to laparotomy or thoracotomy rather than CT.[^3_6][^3_5]


## Operating-room prioritization

In South African level-1 trauma practice, the attending trauma surgeon prioritizes emergency surgery primarily by **hemodynamics**, in discussion with anesthesia. A South African emergency-surgery triage system uses: **red = immediate life-saving operation with simultaneous resuscitation**, **orange = operation within 1–2 hours after resuscitation**, and **green = necessary but not immediately life- or limb-saving within 24 hours**.[^3_13]


| Category | Typical Bara-type examples | Time target |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Red / immediate** | Unstable penetrating abdominal trauma with positive FAST; cardiac tamponade; massive haemothorax; exsanguinating pelvic or vascular injury | Theatre during active resuscitation. [^3_13][^3_6] |
| **Orange / expedited** | Stable patient with penetrating abdominal injury and peritonitis; threatened limb ischemia; deteriorating head injury needing decompression | Within 1–2 hours. [^3_13] |
| **Green / emergent** | Closed fracture requiring fixation, abscess drainage, or other non-life-threatening surgical problem | Within 24 hours. [^3_13] |

## Practical Bara rule

At Bara, the practical hierarchy is usually:

1. **Airway and breathing**—can this patient oxygenate and ventilate?
2. **Circulation**—is there compressible or non-compressible hemorrhage, and is the patient responding?
3. **Neurologic status**—is there a brain or spinal-cord threat?
4. **Anatomy and mechanism**—penetrating torso, multiple cavities, major vascular injury, or severe head injury raise urgency.
5. **Response to resuscitation**—persistent acidosis, rising lactate, or no response to blood pushes the patient to theatre.
6. **Resource triage**—when several critical patients arrive together, the surgeon assigns the scarcest resources—blood, theatre, ICU bed, senior surgeon—to those most likely to die without immediate intervention.[^3_2][^3_8][^3_13]
